Commercial Plumbing Code Basics Every Carson Business Owner Should Know

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Commercial plumbing operates under a meaningfully different set of code requirements than a standard residential job, and a lot of business owners only learn the specifics when something fails or an inspection flags an issue. Understanding the basics ahead of time makes it easier to stay compliant and avoid surprises.

Backflow Prevention Is Not Optional

California's Title 17 of the Code of Regulations requires backflow prevention assemblies to be tested at least annually by a certified tester, statewide, with no exceptions for property type once a backflow assembly is present. Backflow preventers exist to stop contaminated water from a commercial property's internal plumbing from flowing backward into the public water supply, a real risk anywhere chemicals, industrial processes, or even just a large irrigation system are connected to the water line.

This applies broadly across commercial, industrial, and multi-residential properties, and a failed or overdue test can result in fines or, in some cases, water service restrictions until the issue is resolved. If you're not sure whether your property has a backflow assembly or when it was last tested, that's worth confirming rather than assuming someone else is tracking it.

Grease Interceptors for Food Service

Any commercial kitchen, restaurant, cafe, or food prep space is generally required to have a properly sized grease interceptor, sometimes called a grease trap, to prevent fats, oils, and grease from entering the sewer system and causing blockages downstream. Interceptors need regular pumping and maintenance, and neglecting this is one of the more common ways a food service business ends up with an emergency backup that could have been avoided with routine service.

Fixture Counts and ADA Requirements

Commercial properties have specific requirements for the number of restroom fixtures relative to occupancy, along with accessibility requirements under the ADA for at least some fixtures in most commercial spaces. This matters most during a buildout or major renovation, since getting fixture counts wrong at the planning stage means expensive rework later rather than a simple fix.

Water Heater Sizing for Commercial Use

A commercial water heater serving a break room, restroom, or larger occupancy needs to be sized for actual commercial demand, which is a different calculation than a residential household's typical hot water use. Undersizing is a common issue when a residential-grade unit gets installed in a commercial space without accounting for the higher and more concentrated demand a business generates.

Planning a Buildout or Tenant Improvement?

If you're opening a new location or doing a significant renovation, coordinating commercial plumbing planning early in the process, alongside your contractor and before walls and flooring are finished, avoids the more expensive scenario of retrofitting plumbing after the fact. This is especially true for anything involving new gas appliances, since gas line work for commercial kitchen equipment has its own load calculations and code requirements separate from a standard residential gas line.

We handle this kind of code-compliant commercial work throughout Compton as well, where a meaningful share of our commercial calls come from properties with the same backflow and grease interceptor requirements described here.

A Quick Compliance Checklist

  • Confirm whether your property has a backflow assembly and when it was last tested.
  • If you run a food service business, verify your grease interceptor is on a regular maintenance schedule.
  • Before any buildout or renovation, confirm fixture counts and accessibility requirements early in planning.
  • Make sure any water heater serving your business is sized for actual commercial demand, not a residential estimate.

None of this replaces a conversation with your specific jurisdiction's building department for anything project-specific, but it's a solid starting point for understanding what commercial plumbing compliance actually involves before an inspection or a failure forces the issue.

Scheduled Maintenance vs. Reactive Repairs

Commercial properties generally save money over time by moving from a reactive repair model to a scheduled maintenance approach, particularly for the items with real compliance stakes like backflow assemblies and grease interceptors. A missed backflow test doesn't just risk a fine, it risks a water service interruption that affects your entire operation, not just the plumbing. Building these checks into a recurring calendar, rather than responding only when something fails or an inspector flags it, is the difference between predictable, budgeted maintenance and unpredictable, urgent expense.

Working With Property Managers

For commercial tenants who don't own their building, plumbing responsibilities are often split between tenant and property owner in ways that aren't always obvious from a standard lease. Backflow testing responsibility, in particular, sometimes falls on the property owner even when the tenant operates the specific fixtures being tested. If you're unsure where that line falls for your specific lease, clarifying it before a compliance deadline arrives avoids the awkward situation of a missed test with neither party having arranged it.

Keeping Records

Whichever party is responsible, keeping copies of every backflow test result, grease interceptor service record, and any plumbing permit on file in one place makes future inspections, lease renewals, and property sales meaningfully easier. A business that can produce a clean compliance history on request looks better to an inspector, a landlord, and a future buyer than one scrambling to reconstruct records after the fact.

Emergency Preparedness for Commercial Properties

Beyond routine compliance, it's worth knowing where your property's main water and gas shutoffs are located before an emergency makes that knowledge urgent. In a multi-tenant building, this sometimes means coordinating with other tenants or the property manager to make sure everyone knows the shutoff locations and who has access to them outside normal business hours. A commercial plumbing emergency at 2 a.m. is a much smaller problem when someone on-site already knows exactly where the shutoff is than when that information has to be tracked down while water is actively spreading.
