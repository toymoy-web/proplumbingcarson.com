Is It Safe to Smell Gas Near Your Water Heater? What to Do Immediately

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Natural gas is deliberately given a distinct, unpleasant odor, often described as similar to rotten eggs, specifically so that a leak is noticeable. If you smell this near your water heater or anywhere else in your home, the answer to whether it's safe to stick around and investigate is simple: no, it isn't, and the right response is the same regardless of how strong or faint the smell seems.

What to Do Immediately

Leave the property right away. Don't take time to investigate the source, don't open windows on your way out, and don't operate any light switches, appliances, or anything else electrical, since a spark from a switch is a genuine ignition risk in the presence of a gas leak. Get yourself, and anyone else in the home, outside and to a safe distance.

Once you're safely away from the property, call for help. Don't go back inside to grab a phone if you don't already have one with you, use a neighbor's phone or call once you're clearly clear of the building.

Why Water Heaters Specifically

Gas water heaters are one of the more common places a household first notices a gas smell, simply because the water heater is a gas-burning appliance with multiple connection points: the gas supply line itself, the shutoff valve, and the connections at the unit's control valve and burner assembly. Any of these can develop a leak, whether from a loose fitting, a failing valve, or, less commonly, actual damage to the line itself.

What Causes a Gas Leak at a Water Heater

The most common causes are surprisingly mundane: a fitting that was never fully tightened during a past installation or repair, a flexible gas connector that's degraded with age, or a valve that's simply worn out after years of service. Less commonly, physical damage to a line, from something bumping into it during other work nearby, can create a leak where none existed before.

What Happens When a Professional Arrives

A technician responding to a suspected gas leak will use a gas detector to locate the exact source before touching anything, rather than guessing based on where the smell seems strongest, since gas can travel and pool in ways that make the apparent source misleading. Once located, the fix depends on what's actually failed: tightening or replacing a fitting, replacing a degraded connector, or, in more involved cases, repairing or replacing a section of the gas line itself.

Every repair is pressure tested afterward to confirm the leak is fully resolved before the gas supply is restored and the water heater is put back into service. This isn't a step that gets skipped or shortened, given what's at stake if a repair doesn't fully hold.

New Installations and Gas Safety

This same rigor applies to new installations, not just repairs. Any new gas line work, whether for a new water heater, a range, or another appliance, is sized correctly for the total gas load and pressure tested before anything is connected, which is exactly the process that should have happened with the original installation in the first place.

If your gas water heater is also due for a checkup unrelated to any smell, general water heater service includes inspecting connections as a standard part of the visit, which is one more reason not to skip routine maintenance on a gas appliance.

We treat suspected gas leaks with the same urgency across our entire coverage area, including the port-adjacent industrial properties throughout Wilmington where gas infrastructure often serves both residential and commercial accounts in close proximity.

What Not to Do

  • Don't try to locate the leak yourself by sniffing around fittings or connections.
  • Don't operate any light switch, appliance, or garage door opener on your way out.
  • Don't light a match or use any open flame to test for gas, this has genuinely happened and it's extremely dangerous.
  • Don't go back inside until a professional has confirmed it's safe to do so.

A gas smell is one of the few plumbing-adjacent situations where the right response is always the same: leave, then call. There's no version of this where staying to investigate is the safer choice.

What If You're Not Sure It's Actually Gas?

Some people hesitate to call because they're not fully confident the smell is really gas, worried about overreacting to something that turns out to be nothing. This hesitation is understandable, but it's worth setting aside. The odorant added to natural gas is specifically designed to be noticeable and distinct precisely so that people recognize it, and treating a genuine possibility as an emergency costs you a phone call and a few minutes outside. Treating an actual leak as a false alarm carries far more serious risk. When in doubt, always err toward leaving and calling.

Carbon Monoxide: A Related but Separate Concern

Gas leaks and carbon monoxide exposure are related topics but not the same thing. A gas leak is a fuel escaping before combustion, with its own distinct odor. Carbon monoxide is a byproduct of incomplete combustion in a gas appliance that's not venting properly, and it has no odor at all, which is why carbon monoxide detectors are a genuinely important safety device separate from simply being able to smell a raw gas leak. If your home has gas appliances, including a gas water heater, working carbon monoxide detectors are worth having regardless of whether you've ever smelled an actual gas leak.

Routine Checks That Help Catch Problems Early

Beyond responding correctly to an active smell, a periodic visual check of visible gas connections, looking for corrosion, loose fittings, or damage to a flexible connector, can sometimes catch a developing problem before it becomes a full leak. This isn't a substitute for professional inspection, but it's a reasonable habit alongside routine water heater maintenance, especially for a unit that's several years old and hasn't had its connections checked recently.
