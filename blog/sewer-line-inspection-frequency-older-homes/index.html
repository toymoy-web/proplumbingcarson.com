How Often Should You Get Your Sewer Line Inspected in an Older Carson Home?

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

A sewer lateral, the pipe that runs from your home to the city's main sewer line, is easy to ignore because it works fine until suddenly it doesn't. Unlike a leaking faucet or a running toilet, a sewer line problem usually develops slowly underground for years before it produces a symptom anyone notices inside the house.

Why Age and Material Matter More Than a Fixed Schedule

There's no single inspection interval that applies to every home, because the right answer depends heavily on what the sewer lateral is actually made of and how old it is. A home with a modern plastic sewer lateral, installed within the last 20 to 25 years, has a much lower baseline risk than a home still running on the original clay or cast-iron lateral installed when the house was built.

Clay pipe, common in homes built through the mid-20th century, is durable in some respects but has joints every few feet where sections connect, and those joints are exactly where tree roots find their way in, drawn by the moisture inside a pipe with even a small gap or crack. Cast iron corrodes from the inside over decades in a similar way to galvanized water supply pipe, gradually narrowing the usable diameter until flow becomes restricted.

A Realistic Inspection Schedule

For a home with a clay or cast-iron sewer lateral older than 40 years, a camera inspection every two to three years is a reasonable baseline, sooner if you've already had one backup or slow drain issue traced to the main line. For a home with a plastic lateral installed more recently, inspecting after any major landscaping work involving digging, or if you notice early warning signs, is usually sufficient without a fixed recurring schedule.

It's also worth getting an inspection any time you're buying a home with an unknown sewer line history, since a lateral that's about to fail often gives no visible warning from inside the house until the problem is already significant.

Warning Signs Worth an Inspection Right Away

  • More than one fixture in the house backing up within the same day or two.
  • A gurgling sound from one fixture when you run water at another.
  • An unusually green, lush, or soggy patch of lawn with no other explanation.
  • A sunken or settling area in the yard along the likely path of the sewer line.
  • Recurring clogs that come back within weeks of being snaked.

Any one of these on its own is worth a look. Two or more together are a stronger signal that the issue is the main line rather than a single fixture.

What a Camera Inspection Actually Tells You

A proper sewer line inspection uses a camera fed through the line to show its actual condition: root intrusion, cracks, a partial collapse, or just a buildup of grease and debris coating the interior without any structural damage. Knowing which of these you're dealing with matters, because the fix for each is completely different. Root intrusion in an otherwise sound pipe can often be cleared with hydro jetting, while a genuinely collapsed section usually needs a repair or replacement of that portion of line.

Properties near Compton Creek in particular, within our Compton service area, see a slightly higher rate of root-related sewer issues given the moisture drawn toward that corridor, which is one more reason regular inspection makes sense for older homes in that specific area.

Before You Schedule an Inspection

  • Find out your sewer lateral's approximate age and material if you can, from a prior inspection report, home inspection, or city permit records.
  • Note any recurring drain issues, even minor ones, from the past year or two.
  • Check your yard for any of the warning signs listed above.
  • If you've had recent tree work or root removal, mention it, since roots can regrow into an existing crack.

Having this information ready means the inspection can focus on confirming or ruling out a specific concern, rather than starting from scratch.

Inspection Cost vs. Emergency Backup Cost

A routine camera inspection is a small, predictable expense compared to what an actual sewer backup typically costs once it happens. A backup that reaches the point of affecting the lowest drains in a house often means cleanup, in addition to the plumbing repair itself, and if the backup happens while you're away or overnight, the water damage has more time to spread before anyone notices. Viewed against that real cost difference, a periodic inspection on an older lateral is a comparatively small, planned expense against a much larger, unplanned one.

What Happens After an Inspection Finds a Problem

Not every inspection finding requires immediate excavation. A camera inspection that shows early-stage root intrusion in an otherwise structurally sound pipe is often addressed with hydro jetting, clearing the roots and buildup without digging anything up. A finding of significant structural damage, a genuine crack or partial collapse, is a different conversation, and that's usually where trenchless repair options come in, since they can often address a damaged section without a full open trench across the yard.

Either way, having the inspection done before a backup forces the issue means you get to choose the repair approach on your own timeline, rather than making that decision during an active emergency.

Keeping Track Between Inspections

Between scheduled inspections, the warning signs listed earlier are worth checking periodically, especially after a particularly wet winter, since heavy rain can accelerate root growth toward any existing crack or loose joint in an older lateral. Keeping a simple record of when your last inspection happened, and what it found, also makes each future inspection more useful, since a technician can compare current footage against what was documented previously to see whether a known issue has progressed.
