Why Does My Water Pressure Drop When Someone Flushes the Toilet?

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

If your shower suddenly goes cold or weak the moment someone flushes a toilet or starts the washing machine elsewhere in the house, you're dealing with a genuinely common plumbing complaint, and it points to a fairly specific set of causes rather than being a mystery.

What's Actually Happening

Your home's plumbing system has a finite capacity to deliver water at full pressure to every fixture simultaneously. When two fixtures draw water at the same time, whatever supply line is feeding both has to split that flow, and if the line is narrower than it should be, either from original undersizing or internal corrosion, the fixture with less demand behind it, often a shower, will notice the drop most.

Corrosion Is the Most Common Cause in Older Homes

In homes with original galvanized steel supply lines, internal corrosion gradually narrows the usable diameter of the pipe over decades. A pipe that started out delivering plenty of flow for multiple fixtures at once slowly loses that capacity as rust and mineral buildup coat the interior, until eventually even normal simultaneous use, someone flushing while you're showering, creates a noticeable pressure drop that wouldn't have happened when the pipe was newer.

It Can Also Be the Incoming Service Line

Sometimes the bottleneck isn't inside the house at all. The underground service line running from the street to your home's main shutoff can develop the same kind of internal narrowing, or in some cases was simply undersized for the home's current fixture count if the house has been added onto or updated since the line was originally installed. A pressure drop from this cause tends to affect the entire house more uniformly, rather than being isolated to specific fixtures.

How to Tell the Difference

If the pressure drop happens only between certain fixtures, say, the upstairs shower and a downstairs toilet, but not others, that pattern often points to a specific section of interior pipe rather than the main service line. If literally every fixture in the house shows the same drop whenever any other fixture runs, the incoming service line is a more likely suspect.

What the Fix Actually Looks Like

If the cause is corroded interior supply lines, a full repipe with copper or PEX resolves the underlying restriction throughout the house, rather than treating it as a fixture-by-fixture problem. If the incoming service line itself is the bottleneck, replacing that specific line addresses the issue at its actual source.

Either way, the starting point is figuring out which one you're actually dealing with, since repiping the interior of a home won't help if the real restriction is in the underground line feeding it, and vice versa. A straightforward pressure test at different points in the system usually settles the question quickly.

If the issue is isolated to just one fixture rather than a house-wide pattern, it's worth ruling out a simpler cause first, like a clogged aerator, which faucet repair can typically resolve in minutes rather than requiring anything more involved.

We see this exact troubleshooting process regularly throughout Torrance as well, where housing stock spans enough different eras that the actual cause varies meaningfully from one property to the next.

Questions Worth Answering Before You Call

  • Does the drop happen between specific fixtures, or affect the whole house equally?
  • Has this always been an issue, or did it develop gradually over time?
  • Do you know your home's approximate age and whether the plumbing has ever been updated?
  • Has the home had any additions or fixture count changes since it was built?

These details help narrow down if you're looking at an interior repiping conversation or a service line issue before a technician even starts testing.

Does Pressure-Boosting Equipment Help?

For a home with a confirmed service line limitation rather than an interior pipe problem, a pressure booster pump is sometimes suggested as a fix. This can help in specific situations, but it's worth understanding it addresses low overall pressure rather than the multi-fixture drop described here. A booster pump increases pressure across the board, but if the real issue is a narrow section of pipe restricting flow when multiple fixtures draw at once, boosting pressure upstream of that restriction often doesn't fully resolve the simultaneous-use symptom, since the bottleneck itself hasn't gotten any wider.

A Word About Pressure-Reducing Valves

Some homes have a pressure-reducing valve, a PRV, installed near where the main line enters the house, designed to protect plumbing from excessively high street pressure. A failing PRV can occasionally cause pressure fluctuations that mimic the multi-fixture drop described throughout this article, which is one more reason a proper diagnostic test, rather than an assumption based on symptoms alone, is worth doing before committing to a specific repair.

New Construction and Additions

Homes that have had rooms or bathrooms added since original construction sometimes have a pressure problem that traces directly back to that addition. A supply line sized correctly for the home's original fixture count can end up undersized once two or three more fixtures are added downstream of it, even without any corrosion involved at all. If your pressure issues started around the time of a known addition or remodel, mentioning that timeline is a useful clue that can speed up an accurate diagnosis.

Simultaneous Fixture Testing

One of the more useful diagnostic steps a technician can run is a controlled test of exactly which fixture combinations cause the drop and by how much. Running the shower alone and noting the pressure, then flushing a toilet while the shower runs and measuring the change, gives a concrete, repeatable number rather than a subjective impression of "it got weaker." This kind of measured approach also makes it possible to confirm afterward that a repair actually resolved the issue, rather than relying on a general sense that things feel better.
