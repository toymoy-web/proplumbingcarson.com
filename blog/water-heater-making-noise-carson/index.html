Why Is My Water Heater Making Noise? A Carson Homeowner's Guide

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

A water heater that suddenly starts making noise it never made before is usually trying to tell you something specific. The good news is that most of these sounds map to a fairly small list of known causes, and identifying which one you're hearing goes a long way toward knowing whether it's a same-day fix or something that can wait for a scheduled appointment.

Popping or Rumbling: Sediment Buildup

This is by far the most common water heater noise complaint, and it almost always means sediment has settled at the bottom of the tank. As water heats, small pockets of it get trapped under the layer of sediment and turn to steam, and the popping or rumbling sound is that steam pushing its way up through the sediment layer.

Sediment accumulates faster in areas with harder water, which includes most of the Los Angeles basin. A tank that's never been flushed will typically start showing this symptom somewhere around the five to eight year mark, sometimes sooner. A professional flush can clear out lighter buildup, though a tank with years of accumulated sediment sometimes has a layer hard enough that flushing only partially helps, which is worth knowing before assuming a flush will fully resolve the noise.

Ticking or Tapping: Pipe Expansion

A ticking sound, especially right after hot water starts or stops flowing, is often just the sound of pipes expanding and contracting slightly with temperature change. This is common and usually harmless on its own. It becomes worth investigating further if it's a new sound that started suddenly, since that can sometimes indicate a pipe strap has come loose or a section of pipe is rubbing against framing in a way it wasn't before.

Screeching or High-Pitched Whining: Restricted Flow

A high-pitched sound during operation often points to restricted water flow somewhere in the system, sometimes at a partially closed shutoff valve, sometimes from mineral buildup narrowing a fitting near the tank. This is worth checking relatively promptly, since restricted flow puts extra strain on the water heater and can shorten its working life if left unaddressed.

Cracking or Knocking: Loose Heating Element (Electric Units)

For electric water heaters specifically, a cracking or knocking sound during the heating cycle can mean a heating element has come loose or is starting to fail. This isn't something to wait on, since a failing element can eventually trip the unit's safety systems or, in some cases, leak at the element's mounting point.

When Noise Means It's Time for Repair vs. Replacement

A tank under six years old making noise from sediment buildup is generally a good repair candidate, a professional flush plus addressing whatever's causing faster-than-normal buildup usually resolves it. A tank past ten years making the same noise, especially if it's also showing other age-related signs like rust-colored water or a slower recovery time between uses, is often better replaced than repeatedly serviced.

We see this exact pattern regularly across our Long Beach service area too, where a lot of the housing stock was built in the same postwar decades as Carson's older neighborhoods, with water heaters that have been through several replacement cycles since.

If you're not sure which category your unit falls into, a water heater repair visit typically starts with exactly this kind of assessment: is this a fixable issue on a tank with useful life left, or is the unit close enough to the end of its expected service life that replacement makes more sense than continuing to patch it.

What to Check Before You Call

  • Note when the noise started and whether it's gotten louder or more frequent.
  • Check the approximate age of your water heater, often printed on a sticker on the tank itself.
  • Notice whether the noise happens constantly or only during the heating cycle.
  • Check for any other symptoms: discolored water, slower hot water recovery, or visible moisture near the base.

Sharing these details when you call helps narrow down the likely cause before a technician even arrives, which usually means a faster, more accurate fix once they do.

How Often Should a Water Heater Be Flushed?

Flushing a tank, draining it fully to clear out loose sediment, is the main preventive step against the noise and reduced efficiency sediment buildup causes. For a standard tank in a hard water area, an annual flush is the general recommendation, though a tank that's gone several years without one may need more than a single flush to make a real difference, since heavily compacted sediment doesn't always clear out in one pass.

If your tank has never been flushed and is already past the five-year mark, it's worth having a technician assess it in person rather than attempting a DIY flush, since draining a tank with years of heavy sediment buildup can sometimes dislodge material that clogs the drain valve partway through, turning a routine maintenance task into an unplanned repair.

Is the Noise Ever a Sign of a Bigger Problem?

Most water heater noise is exactly what it sounds like, a maintenance issue rather than an emergency. That said, a few combinations are worth treating with more urgency: a loud noise paired with visible moisture around the base of the tank, or a sudden new noise immediately after the unit was serviced or installed.

Both of those combinations point toward something more than routine sediment and are worth a same-day call rather than waiting to see if the noise resolves on its own.

Gas vs. Electric: Does the Noise Differ?

Sediment-related popping and rumbling sounds essentially the same on both gas and electric units, since it's happening inside the tank itself regardless of how the water is heated. Where the two differ is in noise caused by the heating mechanism specifically. A gas unit might produce a soft whooshing sound during ignition, which is normal, while a louder bang at ignition can indicate a delayed ignition problem worth having checked. An electric unit's noises tend to center more on the heating elements themselves, as described above.
