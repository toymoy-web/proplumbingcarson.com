How to Prepare Your Sump Pump Before the Rainy Season

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

A sump pump is one of those pieces of equipment nobody thinks about until the moment they desperately need it to work, usually in the middle of the first serious storm of the season. The frustrating part is that most sump pump failures are preventable with a check that takes less than fifteen minutes.

Test It With Real Water, Not Just a Glance

Looking at a sump pump and confirming it's plugged in tells you almost nothing about whether it actually works. The real test is pouring water directly into the sump basin, enough to trigger the float switch, and confirming the pump activates, moves water out through the discharge line, and shuts off properly once the water level drops. If it doesn't activate, or activates but doesn't seem to be moving water effectively, that's a problem worth addressing well before you actually need it.

Check the Float Switch Specifically

The float switch is the component that tells the pump when to turn on, and it's also one of the more common failure points. Over time, a float switch can get stuck against the side of the basin, preventing it from rising with the water level, or the switch mechanism itself can wear out. During your test, watch the float switch directly to confirm it's moving freely and triggering the pump at the right water level.

Inspect the Discharge Line

The discharge line carries water away from the pump and out of the house, and a blocked or frozen discharge line renders an otherwise working pump useless. Check that the line isn't kinked, blocked by debris, or discharging somewhere that will just let the water flow right back toward your foundation instead of away from it.

Consider a Battery Backup If You Don't Have One

Here's the uncomfortable reality about sump pumps: they're most likely to fail during exactly the conditions that make them most necessary. Serious storms are also the conditions most likely to cause a power outage, and a standard sump pump with no backup power source simply stops working the moment the electricity does. A battery backup system keeps the pump running through an outage, and for any property where pump failure would mean real damage, this is worth serious consideration rather than an optional extra.

Clean Around the Basin

Debris, sediment, and buildup inside the sump basin can interfere with both the float switch and the pump's intake. A quick visual check and cleanout, especially if the pump hasn't run much since last season, helps make sure nothing is obstructing normal operation before it's actually needed.

What to Do If Something's Not Working

If your test reveals a problem, whether that's a pump that doesn't activate, a sluggish response, or unusual noise during operation, getting it addressed before the rainy season starts in earnest is far better than discovering the failure during an actual storm. Sump pump repair or installation is generally a straightforward visit when it's scheduled proactively, compared to an emergency call after water has already started accumulating.

If your property has already dealt with chronic dampness beyond what a sump pump alone should be handling, that broader moisture issue is worth addressing through water mitigation as well, since a pump working overtime against a larger drainage problem is treating a symptom rather than the underlying cause.

This kind of pre-season check is common throughout Lomita too, particularly for older homes with crawl spaces that see more groundwater activity during the wetter months.

Pre-Season Checklist

  • Pour water into the basin and confirm the pump activates and shuts off properly.
  • Watch the float switch move freely during the test.
  • Check the discharge line for kinks, blockages, or poor drainage placement.
  • Confirm whether you have a battery backup, and consider adding one if you don't.
  • Clean debris and sediment from around the basin.

Running through this list once a year, ideally a few weeks before the rainy season typically starts, is a small investment against a genuinely costly failure.

How Long Do Sump Pumps Actually Last?

Most sump pumps have a working life of somewhere between 7 and 10 years under normal conditions, though a unit that runs frequently, in a property with chronic groundwater issues, will generally wear out faster than one that only activates occasionally during the heaviest storms. If you don't know your pump's age, checking the manufacturer's label for a date or model number is worth doing during your pre-season check, since a pump nearing the end of its expected life is worth planning to replace proactively rather than waiting for it to fail during an actual storm.

Primary vs. Backup Pumps

Some properties benefit from a two-pump setup: a primary pump handling normal operation, with a secondary backup pump, sometimes battery-powered, sometimes a completely separate water-powered backup system, that activates automatically if the primary fails or can't keep up with an unusually heavy inflow. For properties where a sump pump failure would mean serious consequences, a finished basement, valuable stored belongings, or a history of significant water intrusion, a backup pump adds a real layer of protection beyond what a single unit alone provides, even a well-maintained one.

What to Do If You Don't Have a Sump Pump Yet

If your property has never had a sump pump but you've noticed water intrusion during past storms, or you know your lot sits lower than the surrounding grade, it's worth having an assessment done before assuming one isn't needed. Not every property requires one, but for those that do, installing a system proactively, on your own schedule, is far less stressful than making that decision during an active flooding situation, when your options and timeline are both far more limited.
