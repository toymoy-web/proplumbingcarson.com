Signs You Have a Slab Leak (And Why Waiting Makes It Worse)

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Of all the plumbing problems a homeowner can face, a slab leak is one of the easiest to miss early and one of the most important to catch before it progresses. Because the leaking pipe is encased in concrete, there's no visible drip or puddle to alert you the way a leaking faucet would. The signs are indirect, which is exactly why so many slab leaks go unnoticed for weeks or months.

The Warm Floor Test

If the leak is on your home's hot water line, the concrete slab directly above it often feels noticeably warmer than the surrounding floor. This is one of the more reliable early signs, and it's worth checking periodically, especially in rooms with tile or other hard flooring where temperature differences are easier to feel underfoot than through carpet.

The Sound of Running Water

If you can hear water running somewhere in the house when every fixture is turned off, that's a strong signal water is escaping somewhere in your plumbing system, and a slab leak is one of the more common sources. This sound is sometimes faint and easy to dismiss, particularly at night when it's the only unusual noise in an otherwise quiet house.

A Water Bill That Doesn't Match Your Usage

A slab leak can lose a meaningful amount of water continuously, all of it going straight into the ground beneath your foundation rather than anywhere visible. If your household's water usage hasn't changed but your bill has climbed noticeably, that's worth investigating, especially if you've also noticed any of the other signs described here.

Cracks That Weren't There Before

In more advanced cases, a slab leak that's gone unaddressed for a long time can cause the soil beneath the foundation to shift as it becomes saturated, and that shifting sometimes shows up as new cracks in tile, flooring, or nearby drywall. This is a later-stage sign, and it's a clear indicator that waiting has already allowed the problem to escalate beyond a straightforward plumbing fix.

Why This One Actually Gets Worse With Time

A lot of plumbing problems are inconvenient but stable, a slow drain isn't getting meaningfully worse while you decide when to deal with it. A slab leak is different. The leak itself doesn't heal or slow down on its own, and the longer water saturates the soil under your foundation, the greater the risk of the kind of structural settling described above. What starts as a plumbing repair can, if ignored long enough, become a foundation conversation instead, which is a significantly bigger and more expensive undertaking.

How It Gets Found and Fixed

Acoustic listening equipment and pressure testing locate a slab leak's position precisely, without needing to break up concrete on a guess. Once found, slab leak repair typically comes down to two options: accessing the pipe directly through a targeted cut in the slab, or rerouting the water line through an accessible wall or ceiling path instead, which avoids breaking concrete altogether.

If you're not yet sure whether what you're noticing is actually a slab leak specifically, starting with general leak detection is a reasonable first step, since the same acoustic and pressure-testing approach applies to narrowing down the leak's location and type before committing to a specific repair path.

We see slab leaks regularly throughout Torrance's slab-on-grade neighborhoods too, many of which share the same postwar construction timeline as Carson's own housing stock.

Quick Self-Check

  • Feel your floors periodically for any unusually warm spot, especially over concrete slab areas.
  • Listen for running water when everything in the house is off.
  • Compare recent water bills against your household's actual usage patterns.
  • Note any new cracks in flooring, tile, or nearby walls.

Catching a slab leak early, before it reaches the structural stage, is almost always the difference between a contained repair and a much larger project.

What Causes a Slab Leak in the First Place

Slab leaks generally trace back to one of a few causes. Corrosion is the most common in older homes, the same gradual internal rusting that affects other galvanized supply lines eventually weakens a section running under the slab until it fails. Ground shifting, whether from natural soil movement or the settling that happens over decades under any foundation, can also stress a pipe at a joint or fitting until it develops a leak.

Less commonly, a slab leak traces back to the original installation itself, a fitting that wasn't seated properly, or abrasion where a pipe rubs against gravel or another hard surface as the ground shifts slightly around it over years. Understanding which of these caused your specific leak matters less for the immediate repair than it does for deciding whether other sections of under-slab plumbing are at similar risk.

Insurance and Slab Leaks

Some homeowner policies cover slab leak repair or the resulting damage, though coverage varies significantly by policy and often depends on the cause. A sudden slab leak is more likely to be treated favorably than one that developed slowly and went unaddressed for a long time, which is one more reason not to delay once you suspect a problem. Documentation from a professional diagnosis, when the leak was found and what caused it, is worth keeping regardless of how your specific claim turns out.

Reroute or Repair: How That Decision Gets Made

Once a slab leak is located, the choice between accessing it directly and rerouting the line isn't arbitrary. Rerouting tends to make more sense when the existing under-slab line is old enough that other sections are likely to fail eventually anyway, since it removes the pipe from the equation entirely rather than fixing one point on a line still aging elsewhere. Direct repair can make sense for a newer system with an isolated, unusual failure, where the rest of the plumbing has no particular reason to be at similar risk. A technician walking through this reasoning with you, rather than defaulting to one option automatically, is a sign of a properly considered recommendation.
