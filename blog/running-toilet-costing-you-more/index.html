Running Toilet? Why It's Costing You More Than You Think

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

A toilet that runs for a few minutes after every flush, or worse, runs continuously, is one of those problems that's easy to live with because it's not dramatic. It doesn't flood anything. It doesn't smell. It just quietly wastes water in the background, which is exactly why it's worth taking seriously rather than ignoring.

How Much Water Is Actually Wasted

A toilet running continuously can waste several hundred gallons of water in a single day, depending on how severe the leak is. Even a toilet that only runs briefly after each flush, rather than continuously, adds up meaningfully across dozens of flushes a week. Over a billing cycle, this can show up as a noticeably higher water bill with no other obvious explanation.

The Two Most Common Causes

Nearly every running toilet comes down to one of two parts failing to seal properly. The flapper, a rubber or silicone seal at the bottom of the tank, is supposed to drop back into place after each flush and block water from continuing to drain into the bowl. Over time, flappers warp, crack, or develop mineral buildup along the sealing edge, all of which prevent a tight seal.

The fill valve is the other common culprit. This valve controls how the tank refills after a flush, and when it fails, it can either run continuously trying to reach a water level it never quite hits, or cycle on and off intermittently even when no one has flushed.

A Simple Test You Can Do Yourself

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank water, wait about 15 minutes without flushing, then check the bowl. If color has appeared in the bowl without a flush, water is leaking past the flapper, confirming that's the source of the problem rather than the fill valve.

Why This Isn't Just a Minor Annoyance

Beyond the direct water waste, a running toilet puts the fill valve and related components under more continuous strain than they're designed for, which can shorten their working life and occasionally lead to a bigger failure, like a fill valve that sticks open entirely and overflows the tank. There's also a simple practical reason to fix it promptly: it's almost always an inexpensive repair, while ignoring it just continues adding to your water bill every single day it runs.

When to Repair vs. Replace the Whole Toilet

For most homes, replacing the flapper or fill valve resolves the issue completely, and both are inexpensive parts that a toilet repair visit typically handles in one trip. Replacement of the entire toilet makes more sense when the unit is significantly older, particularly a pre-1994 model using far more water per flush than current standards, or when the tank or bowl itself has a crack rather than just a failing internal part.

If you're noticing reduced water pressure elsewhere in the house at the same time as a running toilet, it's worth mentioning both when you call, since in some older homes a water line issue affecting overall pressure can make an already-marginal fill valve perform even more erratically.

This is a steady source of calls throughout our Long Beach coverage area too, where a lot of the housing stock is old enough that original toilet hardware has simply worn out from decades of normal use.

Before You Call

  • Try the food coloring test to narrow down whether it's the flapper or the fill valve.
  • Note whether the toilet runs continuously or just briefly after each flush.
  • Check your last one or two water bills for any unexplained increase.
  • Note the toilet's approximate age if you know it.

This is usually enough information for a quick, accurate fix in a single visit.

Why a "Silent" Toilet Can Still Be Wasting Water

Not every running toilet is loud about it. Some leaks are quiet enough that you'd never notice without specifically checking, which is exactly why the food coloring test is worth doing periodically even if nothing seems obviously wrong. A flapper that's just barely failing to seal might only leak a small, steady trickle rather than an audible running sound, but a small steady trickle running continuously for weeks adds up to real water loss even without any noise to alert you.

This is part of why a rising water bill with no other obvious explanation is worth investigating even when every toilet in the house sounds normal. A quiet leak costs just as much as a loud one over time, it's simply easier to overlook.

Multiple Toilets, Multiple Ages

Homes with more than one bathroom sometimes have toilets installed at different times, which means one running toilet doesn't necessarily tell you anything about the others. If one toilet's flapper has failed from age, and both were installed around the same time, it's worth checking the second one too rather than assuming it's fine simply because it isn't currently making noise. Components installed at the same time tend to wear out on similar timelines, even if one shows symptoms before the other.

A Note on Toilet Age and Water Efficiency

Beyond the running issue itself, it's worth knowing that toilets manufactured before 1994 typically use significantly more water per flush than anything sold today, since federal standards changed that year to require more efficient designs. If you're dealing with a toilet old enough to predate that change, a persistent running issue is sometimes a natural point to consider replacement with a current low-flow model rather than continuing to repair a fixture that was inefficient even when it worked correctly. Swapping to a current dual-flush model at that point often pays for itself over a few years in reduced water use alone, separate from whatever the running issue itself was costing.
