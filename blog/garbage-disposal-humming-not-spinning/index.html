Garbage Disposal Humming But Not Spinning? Here's the Fix

By the Carson Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

A garbage disposal that hums when you flip the switch but doesn't actually grind anything is one of the most common kitchen complaints we hear, and the good news is that it's often one of the easier fixes. That humming sound usually means the motor is receiving power and trying to run, but something is physically blocking the impeller plate from spinning.

Why This Happens

Disposals jam when something solid gets wedged between the spinning plate and the grind ring inside the unit. This is often smaller than people expect: a fruit pit, a piece of glass, a bent utensil that fell in unnoticed, or even a buildup of fibrous material like celery strings or potato peels that wound tightly around the impeller shaft.

When that happens, the motor still has power and tries to turn, which produces the humming sound, but the physical obstruction prevents any actual rotation. Left running in this state for too long, the motor can overheat and trip the unit's internal safety switch, which shuts things down entirely rather than continuing to hum.

How to Check It Safely

Before doing anything else, turn off the disposal at the switch and, ideally, at the breaker as well. Never put your hand inside a disposal, even one that's switched off, since a stuck plate can sometimes release unexpectedly.

Most disposals have a small hexagonal hole on the underside of the unit, designed for exactly this situation. Insert an Allen wrench (often included with the unit, or a standard 1/4 inch hex key works for most models) and turn it firmly back and forth. This manually rotates the impeller plate and often works whatever is jammed free without needing to reach inside at all.

The Reset Button

Once you've worked the plate free with the Allen wrench, look for a small red or black reset button on the bottom of the unit. Press it firmly, this resets the internal overload switch that tripped when the motor was straining against the jam. Restore power at the breaker, then try the switch again.

When It's Not a Simple Jam

If the wrench won't turn at all, the obstruction may be more serious, or the impeller itself may have failed. If the unit is silent rather than humming when you flip the switch, that usually points to an electrical issue rather than a mechanical jam, a tripped breaker, a wiring problem, or, on an older unit, a failed motor.

A disposal that jams repeatedly even after clearing it is also worth having looked at, since recurring jams sometimes mean a bent or damaged impeller blade that will keep catching debris until it's replaced. This is also a good moment to check for a related slow drain issue, since a disposal working harder than it should sometimes means the drain line downstream is partially restricted too.

Repair or Replace?

Most disposals last 8 to 12 years with normal use. A unit within that range with an isolated jam is almost always worth clearing rather than replacing. A disposal well past that age, especially one that's jammed more than once recently or has started leaking from the bottom, is usually a better candidate for replacement than continued troubleshooting.

This is one of the more common kitchen calls we get from Lomita homes as well, where a lot of the original kitchen plumbing dates back to the same postwar construction period as much of the rest of the South Bay.

Before You Call

  • Try the Allen wrench method described above before assuming the unit needs replacement.
  • Check for the reset button and press it after clearing any jam.
  • Note whether the unit hums (mechanical jam) or is completely silent (likely electrical).
  • Check the unit's approximate age if you know it.

This information helps a technician quickly tell if you're looking at a five-minute fix or something that needs a closer look.

Preventing the Next Jam

A few habits go a long way toward reducing how often a disposal jams in the first place. Running cold water, not hot, while the disposal is operating helps solidify grease and fats so they get chopped and flushed through rather than coating the inside of the unit. Feeding food in gradually rather than all at once gives the impeller room to actually grind material instead of getting overwhelmed.

Fibrous items are worth avoiding entirely if you can help it. Celery, corn husks, onion skins, and similar stringy material are among the most common causes of a wound, jammed impeller, since the fibers wrap around the spinning mechanism rather than getting chopped cleanly. Bones, fruit pits, and glass or metal that accidentally falls in are the other major category, and none of these belong in a disposal regardless of how sturdy the unit seems.

If jams are becoming a recurring pattern despite reasonable use habits, that's often a sign the unit itself is aging out, rather than a sign you're doing something wrong. A disposal nearing the end of its service life tends to jam more easily on material that wouldn't have caused a problem when the unit was newer and the blades were sharper.

A Word on DIY Repairs

The Allen wrench and reset button steps described above are safe and standard for anyone to try. Going further than that, opening the unit itself or attempting to replace internal components, is a different matter, since disposals involve both electrical connections and sharp blades in a confined space. If the basic steps don't resolve the issue, that's the point to call for professional service rather than continuing to troubleshoot further on your own, since forcing a jammed unit repeatedly can sometimes cause more damage than the original obstruction itself.
