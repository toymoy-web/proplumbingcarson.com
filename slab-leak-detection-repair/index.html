Not Sure What's Wrong?

Warm Floor Spot or Rising Water Bill? It May Be a Slab Leak

A slab leak happens when a water line running underneath a home's concrete foundation develops a leak, usually from corrosion, ground shifting, or an original installation defect. Because the pipe is encased in concrete, the leak often isn't visible directly, showing up instead as a warm patch on the floor (if it's the hot water line), a sound of running water when nothing's on, or a steady rise in the water bill.

Left unaddressed, a slab leak can erode the soil beneath the foundation over time and, in some cases, contribute to cracking in the slab itself. It's a problem worth addressing promptly once suspected, not something to monitor and wait on.

Signs You Need This Service

Signs of a Slab Leak

🌡️

Warm Area on the Floor

If the leak is on the hot water line, the slab above it often feels noticeably warmer than the surrounding floor.

🔊

Sound of Running Water

Hearing water running when every fixture in the house is off is a strong indicator of a leak somewhere in the system, including possibly under the slab.

💵

Unexplained High Water Bill

A slab leak can lose a significant amount of water continuously without any visible sign inside the home.

🧱

Cracks in Flooring or Walls

In more advanced cases, shifting caused by a slab leak can show up as new cracks in tile, flooring, or nearby walls.

In Carson, CA

Slab Leaks in Carson's Slab-Foundation Homes

Many homes built in Carson from the mid-20th century onward use slab-on-grade foundations, which means water supply lines run beneath the concrete rather than through an accessible crawl space. That construction style is common and generally reliable, but it means a leak in one of those buried lines requires either accessing it through the slab or rerouting the line entirely, since simply patching isn't possible without exposing the pipe.

We use acoustic detection equipment to pinpoint a slab leak's location before recommending a repair approach, since guessing at the location and breaking concrete in the wrong spot adds unnecessary cost and disruption. Once located, there are usually two paths: accessing the specific point through the slab to repair that section of pipe, or rerouting the water line entirely through an accessible wall or ceiling path, bypassing the slab altogether.

Rerouting is often the less disruptive option, especially for older homes where the existing under-slab pipe material is likely to develop additional leaks elsewhere along its length in the coming years anyway. We'll walk through both options with real cost and disruption tradeoffs specific to your home's layout.

Pricing Transparency

What Affects the Price of Slab Leak Detection & Repair

We can't give an accurate number without seeing the job, but these are the real factors that move the price up or down.

Direct repair vs. rerouting

Rerouting through an accessible wall or ceiling path sometimes costs less than breaking through concrete, depending on the home's layout.

Length of the reroute path

A short reroute costs less than one that has to travel a long distance to reach a fixture.

Flooring above the slab

Tile or stone flooring adds cost to a direct-access repair compared to carpet or vinyl.

On insurance: A slab leak that developed suddenly is sometimes covered, particularly the resulting damage, though coverage for gradual, long-term leaks is far less consistent across carriers. We document what we find either way.
